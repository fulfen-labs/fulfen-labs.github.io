Do you need a forward deployed engineer?

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) works inside your organisation, beside the people who will use an AI system, writes production code against your real data and constraints, and is judged by what your team can do after they leave [handbook ch. 1]. That is expensive help, and plenty of organisations do not need it. This page helps you find out, in a few minutes, which kind of help you need, if any.

Answer for one AI system or outcome you have in mind. There are four short parts:

  1. Should this use AI at all? (Part 1)
  2. Do you need outside help to get it into production? (Part 2)
  3. Who will own it afterwards? (Part 3)
  4. For leaders of companies that sell a product: should you build an FDE function of your own? (Part 4)

Each part ends with an honest outcome and the handbook chapter that teaches it. "You do not need an FDE" is one of them, and so is "build your own".

Part 1. Should this use AI at all?

Sometimes the most useful answer is not to use AI [handbook ch. 9]. Answer Yes or No.

1bWould a mistake cost more than the people doing the work now, before the system has proved itself in evaluation?Earn trust through evaluation first, with a person deciding until then
1cIs the data it needs missing, or out of reach?If it is missing: not feasible until it exists; fix the data first, because an AI system without the data invents. If it exists but access is not approved: not yet, blocked pending a decision on access

Outcome. Any Yes: not with AI, or not yet, in the form the table gives. The same six conditions lead to the same verdicts in the readiness self-assessment (its Part 1c and Part 4). That is a finished answer, not a failure: saying so in the first week is cheaper than in the third month. Read chapter 9 ("Start by Not Using AI") and chapter 8 (the honest feasibility report) [handbook ch. 8, 9]. All No: go on to Part 2.

Part 2. Do you need outside help to get it into production?

The question is not "can we hire an FDE?" It is how long would it take us to work all of this out ourselves, compared with working beside someone who has done it before? [handbook ch. 1]. Three questions help you answer it.

2a. How far along is your team? Has your organisation already taken an AI system into production, with engineers who build and run it, evaluation and release gates, and a security team that has approved it?

  • Further along: yes, at least once.
  • Earlier on: not yet, or only as demos and pilots.

The page's own test: the handbook speaks of digital maturity in general; this page narrows it to one checkable question, whether you have taken an AI system into production before.

2b. How much shaping does the system need? Does it have to be fitted closely to your work, your data, your systems and your rules, or can it be used largely as it comes?

  • Heavy shaping: much of the work is fitting it to how you work.
  • Light shaping: it does a standard job in a standard way.

The handbook plots these two against each other [handbook ch. 1]:

Light shapingHeavy shaping
Further alongYou do not need an FDE. Self-service, with good documentation, is enoughLight help may be enough: an experienced engineer who advises and unblocks your team, rather than one embedded full time
Earlier onYou do not need an FDE. A standard rollout, closer to ordinary onboarding, is enoughAn FDE fits. This is the case embedded work is for

2c. Are the foundations there? Do you have a data platform the system can draw on, an engineering team who will work on it, and a security function that can approve new connections to your systems?

  • Yes, all three: keep the answer from the grid.
  • No, one or more is missing: foundations first. Without them there is nothing to connect the system to, and the first piece of work becomes building those foundations. The handbook treats this as a reason to scope the work honestly, not to give up [handbook ch. 1, 8].

Outcome. Read chapter 1 ("Do You Need an FDE?" and "FDE Versus Everything Else") for the four cases and the neighbouring roles (software engineer, solutions architect, technical consultant, sales engineer) that may suit you better. If foundations come first, chapter 8 shows how to discover what your systems, data and tools can support [handbook ch. 1, 8].

How this page reads the handbook: chapter 1's grid is drawn for a vendor's product, plotting the customer's digital maturity against the product's customisability. Because this page is vendor-neutral, it asks about the system you want to build, whatever it is built on, and words the two axes as "how far along" and "how much shaping".

Part 3. Who will own it afterwards?

An FDE is temporary by design. The test of the work is what your team can do after the FDE leaves; a team that cannot run the system alone is the "permanent crutch" the handbook warns against [handbook ch. 1].

3a. When the system is running, do you want your own people to run it, handle its incidents and improve it?

3b. Do you expect to build more than one system like it? (The page's own question: the handbook's measure is the first system, but a second makes a team of your own easier to justify.)

Outcome. Yes to either: build your own team. The handbook's name for it is a pod: a small, permanent team of your own, with three responsibilities between them: one person who owns the domain and the requirements, one who builds and maintains the system and what surrounds it, and one who owns evaluation, monitoring and reliability. The pod is yours and permanent; an FDE, if you use one, is temporary, and their job is to stand the pod up and hand over to it, until they are no longer needed. Read chapter 26 (the agentic pod, the autonomy ladder and the three handover milestones) and chapter 1 ("The Pod and the FDE") [handbook ch. 1, 26]. The handbook sets out the full pod for systems of medium to high complexity; for a smaller one, two people, or even one, can cover the three responsibilities. What cannot be skipped is at least one person clearly accountable for the system after the builders leave [handbook ch. 26].

No to both, and nobody on your side would own it: go back to question 1d [handbook ch. 9].

Part 4. For leaders of companies that sell a product: should you build an FDE function?

This part is for a different reader: a leader at a company that sells a product or platform, deciding whether to build a forward deployed team to serve its customers. The handbook sets three preconditions [handbook ch. 27].

#QuestionIf No
4aIs your product technically complex, and sold to buyers who cannot get its value without help?If the buyer is technical, invest in documentation and developer relations; if the product is simple, in self-service or an ordinary sales motion
4bDo you have a platform of shared building blocks that the team assembles from, or the will and budget to build one from what the field teaches you?Without it you have a development shop with a better title, and its upkeep grows with every customer
4cHave you decided what the function is for: services revenue, product bets, or keeping and growing accounts?Decide first. Trying to do several at once fails, because the short-term pull of services revenue drags the team away from the product

Outcome. All three Yes: build your own FDE function. Write its purpose down in a sentence or two and use it to turn away work that does not fit; choose where it reports to match that purpose; and measure it by that purpose's measures, not the easiest ones to collect. Read chapter 27 (the FDE lead, the first three hires, quality standards and the pattern library) and chapter 3 (the capability model) [handbook ch. 3, 27].

Any No: not yet, and the "If No" column says what to do instead. One more warning from chapter 27: if your FDEs spend most of their time helping customers adopt a product that should have been easier to adopt, fix the product rather than hire more FDEs [handbook ch. 27].

How this page reads the handbook: chapter 27 writes these preconditions for a company that sells a product. If you are building capability for your own organisation's use, Part 3's pod is usually the question to ask, not this one [handbook ch. 1, 26].
