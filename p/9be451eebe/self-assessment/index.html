Is your AI pilot ready for production?

Demos run on curated data, with cooperative users, no adversaries, unlimited budgets and no regulator watching. Production has none of those conditions, and it does not stand still [handbook ch. 22]. This sheet helps you find out, in about an hour, whether one pilot is ready for it.

You fill it in yourselves. It asks four things in order:

  1. Should this use AI at all? (Part 1)
  2. What does the pilot actually do, and can it do it here? (Part 2)
  3. How ready is it on the six dimensions production needs? (Part 3)
  4. Which of four honest verdicts fits, and what do you do next? (Part 4)

Every block names the handbook chapter it comes from, so each gap you find points to the chapter that teaches how to close it.

Before you start

  • One pilot, one sheet. If the pilot does several jobs, pick the one that matters most and assess that.
  • Who should be in the room. The person who owns the pilot and will act on the verdict; someone who built it; someone who runs it, or will. Bring someone from security if you can: identity and access questions stall more deployments than technical ones, and security approval asked for late is the most common cause of delay [handbook ch. 8].
  • How to answer. Each question in Part 3 takes Yes, Partly or No.
    • Yes means you can point at the evidence now: a test result, a document, a dashboard, a record. The "Yes looks like" column says what that evidence is.
    • A yes you cannot evidence is a partly [handbook appendix D].
    • If nobody in the room knows, answer No, and write down who would know. (This is the sheet's own rule.)
  • Keep moving. Aim for about a minute a question. If one turns into a debate, mark it Partly and come back to it at the end. (This is the sheet's own rule.)
  • Does not apply. If a question, or part of one, cannot apply because of what the pilot does (for example, the write half of a question when Part 2 shows the pilot only reads), write N/A with one sentence saying why. N/A is scored as Yes only when the action table in Part 2 shows the question cannot apply, and someone who did not build the pilot agrees in writing [handbook appendix A, Field Judgement 20]. Otherwise answer No. (This is the sheet's own rule.)

Part 1. Should this use AI at all? [handbook ch. 9]

Part 1 is not scored. It decides whether the rest of the sheet is worth filling in.

1a. The outcome [handbook ch. 9]

Write what the pilot is for in one sentence, using this formula:

[action] + [metric] + [from X to Y] + [for whom] + [by when] + [quality gate]

Our outcome: ______________________________________________________________

If any part of the formula is missing, or any check below is no, the statement is still a wish: do not start building until the metric, population, deadline and quality gate are explicit [handbook ch. 9]. Note what is missing; Part 4 uses it.

CheckYes or no
Measurable: a number someone else could verify
Bounded: a deadline
Attributable: a named population who benefits
Quality-gated: a minimum standard that stops the metric being gamed

1b. The vehicle [handbook ch. 9]

Which of these is the pilot, honestly? Each has a different quality bar. The common mistake is treating the prototype as the product, the product as the pilot and the pilot as production [handbook ch. 9].

VehicleWhat it is forTick one
PrototypeValidating the approach on real data; disposable by design
Minimum viable productThe least useful functionality for real users, at production quality within a narrow scope
PilotA controlled subset of the real environment: real users and data, limited blast radius
Production rolloutThe target population, with full blast radius and accountability

A prototype with a hard-coded credential is acceptable; a product with one is a security incident [handbook ch. 9]. If you ticked Prototype, keep what it proved and expect to rebuild it through the release gates, not to promote it [handbook ch. 6, 9].

1c. Six conditions that make AI the wrong answer [handbook ch. 9]

Read this table the other way round from the rest of the sheet: here, a yes is a warning.

#ConditionDoes it hold here? (yes or no)If it holds
1c.1The rules are known and complete. You could enumerate every case.Ordinary software is more reliable and cheaper to maintain. Verdict: this should not use AI.
1c.2The cost of error exceeds the cost of human labour, and the system has not yet earned trust through evaluation.Earn trust through evaluation first, with a person deciding until then. The verdict cannot be better than "ready with work".
1c.3The data does not exist or cannot be accessed. An agent without data invents.If the data does not exist: not feasible until it does; fix the data first. If it exists but nobody has approved access: not yet, blocked pending a decision.
1c.4Your organisation cannot maintain it. Nobody here could understand, evaluate or update it once its builders move on.You are building a dependency, not a solution. Not yet: blocked pending a decision on who will own it.
1c.5Regulation prohibits it.No amount of engineering overcomes a legal constraint. Verdict: this should not use AI.
1c.6The problem is political, not technical.AI cannot fix turf wars or leadership indecision. Verdict: this should not use AI for this problem.

Start from the simplest pattern that solves the problem: a plain function, then a fixed workflow (with model calls inside it if needed), then a single agent with tools, and only then several agents. Move up only when the level below demonstrably fails [handbook ch. 9; Field Judgement 3].

Part 2. What does it actually do? [handbook ch. 8]

Part 2 is not scored on the yes, partly, no scale. It produces the action table that Part 4 reads.

An agent needs actions, not systems: named operations with inputs, preconditions, effects and a failure mode. "Integrate with the finance system" is not an action; "post a credit memo against an unpaid invoice, within the tolerance, given an approved dispute reason" is [handbook ch. 8]. List one row per action the pilot takes or is meant to take.

Action, named as an operationInterface it runs throughBlast radius classFeasibility stateNeeded for the outcome?If blocked: who must decide

Blast radius classes [handbook ch. 8]

ClassExamplesImplication
Read-onlyQuery, retrieve, summariseRecoverable. Start here
Reversible writeDraft, comment, internal status changeEasy to undo, easy to audit
Irreversible internalPayment posted, record deleted, ledger entryNeeds approval or strong preconditions
Externally visibleEmail to a customer, regulatory filing, funds movedHuman gate until proven, and often after

Feasibility states [handbook ch. 8]

StateMeans
Available nowThe interface exists and access can be granted
Available with workAn integration must be built, but nothing is blocked
Blocked pending decisionSomeone must approve, fund or waive something
Not feasibleNo viable interface

Read actions are rarely on the blocked list; write actions are rarely off it [handbook ch. 8].

Then answer two questions about the table:

  1. Which steps can the pilot perform end to end? Does the work left to people break the case for building it? Answer yes or no: ______ [handbook ch. 8]
  2. What is the pilot's risk class? [handbook ch. 9]
    • Informational: it answers, summarises, finds.
    • Advisory: it recommends; a person decides and acts.
    • State-changing: it acts on systems of record or the outside world. A state-changing system needs everything in Part 3 before its first write [handbook ch. 9].

Blast radius, not model capability, sets how much autonomy the pilot should start with [handbook ch. 8].

Part 3. The six readiness dimensions [handbook ch. 22]

The six dimensions are the handbook's production-readiness checklist: security, reliability, observability, governance, operations, and cost and performance [handbook ch. 22]. Each question comes from that checklist or from the chapter it points to.

Scoring [handbook ch. 17 scale; appendix D evidence rule]

  • Yes = 2, Partly = 1, No = 0.
  • A yes you cannot evidence is a partly.
  • For every Partly or No, write W or D in the last column:
    • W (work): your team can close it by doing the work.
    • D (decision): closing it needs someone else to approve, fund or waive something. This is ch. 8's "blocked pending decision", applied to the gap [handbook ch. 8].

3.1 Security [handbook ch. 20; also ch. 8, 12, 14]

Read: chapter 20, "Identity, Security and Access for Agents".

#QuestionYes looks likeCh.Y / P / NW / D
S1For every action in Part 2, can you say on whose behalf it runs and as whom?An identity design per action: as the user, as itself, or for a user as itself. No action runs under an unowned service account.8, 20
S2Has every permission the pilot does not need been removed, rather than watched?A record of what was removed at each layer: tools, operations within tools, token scopes, data (queries and retrieval run with the user's attributes, tested with an access matrix that includes restricted best matches), time (short-lived credentials) and network (egress denied by default).12, 20
S3Are credentials kept out of the model's context?Credentials injected server-side from a secret store, short-lived and scoped; development and production fully separated; none in prompts, tool parameters or the client.14, 20
S4Can you stop the agent, and have you tried?Revoking the agent's credentials stops every action; it fails closed and offers a person; the date of the last test and how long it took to take effect are recorded.20
S5Have you tested prompt injection, direct and indirect, and checked the lethal trifecta?Injection test cases with results. Where the pilot has private data, untrusted content and a way to communicate externally, the design removes at least one of the three.20
S6Is personal data minimised and masked?Only the personal data the task needs is passed to the model; personal data is redacted or tokenised in traces, logs and evaluation sets.20

3.2 Reliability [handbook ch. 18; also ch. 10, 12, 14, 19]

Read: chapter 18, "Evaluation and Release Gates"; chapter 10 for output contracts, chapter 12 for grounding, chapter 14 for tools.

#QuestionYes looks likeCh.Y / P / NW / D
R1Is there a golden set built from real data, and does the pilot pass it at thresholds you agreed in advance?A versioned set covering normal, edge, adversarial, unauthorised and high-consequence cases, refusals included; results reported by segment, not only in aggregate.18
R2Does a regression suite gate every change?Evaluation runs in continuous integration; a change to instructions, tools, retrieval or model cannot merge without passing, compared pair by pair against the current release.18, 22
R3Is the model's output held to a contract, and is its meaning checked in code?A typed schema with enumerations and nullable fields for absent values; arithmetic, ranges and cross-checks validated in code; failures routed deliberately, not retried blindly.10
R4Are answers grounded, and does the pilot say when it does not know?Checkable citations with source and revision; no-answer behaviour designed and evaluated; live facts fetched from the system of record, not from documents. (No retrieval? Answer for wherever its facts come from.)12
R5Is every call to an external system safe to time out, retry and fail?Timeouts, retries and circuit breakers on every external call; writes made idempotent before any retry; structured errors that tell an empty result from a failure.14, 19
R6What happens when the model or a dependency is unavailable?Behaviour designed and tested: a named fallback evaluated in advance, with every use recorded and alerted on, or the pilot fails closed and offers a person.19, 22

3.3 Observability [handbook ch. 19; also ch. 22]

Read: chapter 19, "Observability, Troubleshooting and Cost".

#QuestionYes looks likeCh.Y / P / NW / D
O1Is every request traced end to end?Traces across model calls, retrieval, tool calls and gates, tied to the business case (the order, the invoice) and the identities involved, and recording the release that produced the output.19, 22
O2Do you know the cost per successful outcome?Tokens and cost accounted per call, request and case, and divided by outcomes that succeeded, not by requests.19
O3Are service levels defined, with an owner and a response for every alert?Availability and latency defined as ratios over a window; a failure budget with alerts on how fast it burns; each alert has a named owner and a written response.19
O4Are the rules that must always hold checked continuously?Invariants (for example, no record changed twice by one operation; no answer citing a document the user may not see) checked deterministically against durable state. A violation is an incident.19
O5Do you watch quality as well as uptime?Sampled review by workflow and segment, with the sampling window and reviewer recorded; trends in escalation, abstention, override and guardrail rates.19, 21
O6Could someone who did not build the pilot diagnose a production issue from its monitoring?Dashboards organised by an operator's questions (is it up, is it right, is it costing what it should, is it being misused); every alert linked to a runbook entry; a known good baseline.22

3.4 Governance [handbook ch. 21; also ch. 10, 22]

Read: chapter 21, "Safety, Governance and Human Oversight".

#QuestionYes looks likeCh.Y / P / NW / D
G1Are the rules that must hold enforced outside the prompt?Limits and preconditions (for example, a refund limit) enforced in code, gates or policy the business owns.21
G2Do approvals match the blast radius of each action?Approval tiers: automatic for reads, context-aware for reversible changes, always for irreversible or externally visible actions; approvals stored durably so a paused run survives a late answer.8, 21
G3Is human review routed by risk, not by the model's confidence?Review triggered by defined risk and validation signals; a sample of cases nobody flagged reviewed by segment.21
G4Could you reconstruct any decision the pilot made?Audit records (what, on whose behalf, as whom, under which rule, approved by whom, when); lineage of every output (inputs, sources, tool results, model, instruction version, configuration); decision logs for the design.21
G5Is the pilot's regulatory classification recorded?The obligations that apply are written down and mapped to design requirements (record-keeping, transparency, human oversight and the rest), raised at the start, not at launch.21
G6Are prompts, configuration and model identifiers under change control?Instructions, tool descriptions, configuration, model identifiers and retrieval settings versioned together as one release, in the repository; models pinned to exact versions, with retirement dates in the operations calendar.10, 22

3.5 Operations [handbook ch. 22; also ch. 26]

Read: chapter 22, "Shipping and Running AI Systems"; chapter 26 for handover.

#QuestionYes looks likeCh.Y / P / NW / D
P1Is deployment automated, with no manual steps?The same release promoted unchanged through development, test, staging and production; configuration validated at start-up, refusing to start if anything is missing.22
P2Has rollback been tested?A rollback rehearsed before go-live, with its time recorded, including what happens to runs in flight and to data the new release wrote.22
P3Are on-call and escalation paths defined?Named people for on-call and escalation; where the people with access are not the people who understand the system, both are paged onto the same call.22
P4Has the incident runbook been rehearsed by the team that will run it?A drill with an incident lead, an operations lead and a communications owner; response and recovery times recorded; runbook corrected afterwards.22, 26
P5Is the environment contract written and agreed?Identity, networking, artefact sources, models, trust and time, capacity and operations, each with evidence and a named owner. "On premises" is not a specification.22
P6Could the team that will run it operate, recover and improve it without its builders?The three handover milestones met or scheduled: a week of steady-state operation without the builders, an incident handled by the team, and an improvement shipped through the gates without help.26

3.6 Cost and performance [handbook ch. 19; also ch. 9, 10, 22]

Read: chapter 19, "Observability, Troubleshooting and Cost"; chapter 10 for model choice.

#QuestionYes looks likeCh.Y / P / NW / D
C1Are budgets per interaction and per run enforced in code?Maximum tokens and cost per interaction and per run, set in the harness rather than the prompt, with a graceful response when a budget runs out.19, 22
C2Does the pilot meet its latency target at the agreed percentile?An agreed target and percentile; latency recorded as a distribution, and the target met at that percentile.19, 22
C3Is the model choice justified against the nearest alternative?One sentence naming the alternative and why it loses here, backed by results on your own evaluation set; a capable model used first to learn what quality is achievable, then a cheaper one only as far as evaluation allows.9, 10
C4Has the cost projection been checked against the budget of whoever will pay to run it?A cost projection, built on measured cost per successful outcome, validated against that budget.19, 22

A dimension where every answer is Yes, or where every gap has a written mitigation accepted by the people who will run the pilot, is ready. Any other dimension is a gap, and the score tells you how large. There is no pass mark: the handbook's checklist is reviewed line by line with the people who will run the system, and signed off before go-live, with every item addressed as a pass, a fail with mitigation, or not applicable [handbook ch. 22; ch. 27, "Minimum Standards"]. Whoever checks the evidence must not be someone who built the pilot [handbook appendix A, Field Judgement 20].

Part 4. The verdict

The rule

Work down this list. The first verdict that applies is yours.

OrderVerdictIt applies if any of these is true
1Not feasible, or "this should not use AI"Condition 1c.1, 1c.5 or 1c.6 holds. Condition 1c.3 holds because the data does not exist. An action the outcome needs is "not feasible" in Part 2 and, in Part 2's first question, the work left to people breaks the case.
2Not yet: blocked pending a decisionCondition 1c.4 holds, or 1c.3 holds because access has not been approved. An action the outcome needs is "blocked pending decision" in Part 2. The outcome in 1a is still a wish (a part missing or a check failed: the sheet's own rule, since only its sponsor can fix it). Any question in Part 3 is marked D.
3Ready with workAnything short of ready that is not caught above: any Partly or No marked W that has no written mitigation accepted by the people who will run the pilot; any action "available with work"; an action "not feasible" whose loss does not break the case (drop it from scope); condition 1c.2 holds; or the vehicle in 1b is a prototype.
4ReadyAll of these: every Part 3 answer is Yes with evidence, or a gap with a written mitigation accepted by the people who will run the pilot; the evidence has been checked by someone who did not build the pilot; every action the outcome needs is "available now"; no condition in 1c holds; the outcome in 1a is complete and passes all four checks; the vehicle ticked in 1b is not a prototype.

The order matters. A pilot that should not use AI is not "blocked"; a blocked pilot is not "ready with work", because no amount of work closes a gap that is waiting for someone's decision.

What to do next

Ready. Sign the checklist off with the people who will run the pilot, not only the people who built it [handbook ch. 22]. Release progressively: run it in shadow, then serve a small share of traffic, and widen only as quality, safety, latency and cost hold; where traffic cannot be split, roll out in order of risk [handbook ch. 22]. Keep this sheet. Fill in a new one after any model, permission or major design change, and compare: the change between two sheets is evidence too [handbook ch. 18, 22; appendix D].

Ready with work.

  1. List every Partly and No, and every action "available with work".
  2. Rank them by risk, using the blast radius of the actions each gap touches [handbook ch. 8]. If the pilot is state-changing, close every Part 3 gap before its first write in production [handbook ch. 9].
  3. Give each item an owner and a date.
  4. If 1c.2 holds, keep a person deciding, and raise autonomy in stages, each a reversible step taken on evidence [handbook ch. 21, 26].
  5. If the pilot is a prototype, keep the specification it proved and rebuild through the release gates [handbook ch. 6, 9].
  6. Use the reading table below to find the chapter for each gap, then fill the sheet in again.

Not yet: blocked pending a decision.

  1. Write every D item and every blocked action as a dependency, with one named owner against each line. A dependency without an owner is a wish [handbook ch. 8].
  2. Take each decision to its owner as a short decision memo: the options, the evidence, and the trigger for revisiting it [handbook ch. 24].
  3. If the missing decision is identity or access, have the access conversation with your security team now: how automated services authenticate, how service identities are audited and revoked, and how long a new identity takes to approve [handbook ch. 8].
  4. If nobody can own the pilot after its builders move on, that is the decision to make first [handbook ch. 9, 26]. If the outcome in 1a is still a wish, the person who sponsors the pilot completes it before anything else is built [handbook ch. 9].
  5. Do not build around a block. Work that can go ahead (marked W) can continue, but the verdict stays "not yet: blocked pending a decision" until the decisions are made.

Not feasible, or "this should not use AI". This is a finished result, not a failure. Write it up: the reasons, what is missing, and what to do instead [handbook ch. 8, 24].

  • If the rules are known and complete, build ordinary software or a fixed workflow [handbook ch. 9].
  • If the data does not exist, fix the data first [handbook ch. 9].
  • If every valuable action is blocked or not feasible, the work is an access and integration project wearing an AI label. It may still be worth doing. It is not the project you thought it was [handbook ch. 8].
  • If regulation or politics is the obstacle, no engineering will move it; take it to the people who can [handbook ch. 9].

Saying "not yet" early is much cheaper than saying it in month three [handbook ch. 8, 24].
